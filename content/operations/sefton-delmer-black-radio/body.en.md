## Overview

Sefton Delmer's black radio was a British wartime propaganda effort in which fake German stations, secretly produced in England, posed as voices from inside the Nazi system. Run by the journalist Sefton Delmer for Britain's secret propaganda apparatus, from August 1941 the Political Warfare Executive (PWE), the stations mixed accurate news and entertainment with invented scandal to erode the morale of German soldiers and civilians.

## Background

Delmer was born in Berlin in 1904 to an Australian father and spoke German like a native. As a Daily Express correspondent he knew leading Nazis, flew with Hitler in the 1932 campaign and walked through the burnt-out Reichstag with him in 1933. According to The National Archives, the PWE was formed in August 1941 from parts of the BBC, the Ministry of Information and SO1, the propaganda branch of the Special Operations Executive. An MI5 file on Delmer records that during the war he "was responsible for psychological warfare broadcast to Germany".

## Methods

- Gustav Siegfried Eins (GS1) first broadcast on 23 May 1941. Its host, "Der Chef", supposedly a loyal old Prussian officer, was voiced by the Berlin exile Peter Seckelmann and denounced the corruption and sexual excesses of Nazi Party officials in obscene language.
- Scripts drew on prisoner-of-war interrogations, intelligence reports and German newspapers so that the gossip sounded authentic.
- Delmer summarised his formula as "cover, cover, dirt, cover, dirt": credible material to win trust, then the damaging "dirt".
- From 1943 the Atlantiksender targeted U-boat crews and Soldatensender Calais presented itself as a station for German soldiers, using the powerful Aspidistra transmitter. They offered real news, sport, dance music and jazz, laced with rumours and, before D-Day, misleading reports about invasion plans.
- According to the Smithsonian, American aircraft dropped an average of 250,000 copies of the newspaper Nachrichten für die Truppe each night around the invasion.

## Exposure

GS1 ended in the autumn of 1943, after some 700 broadcasts, with a staged Gestapo raid in which the Chef was "shot"; accounts differ on the exact date. In November 1943 Joseph Goebbels wrote in his diary that Soldatensender Calais did "a very clever job of propaganda". Official records stayed classified for decades, but Delmer described the operation in his memoir Black Boomerang (1962).

## Legacy

The real impact is hard to measure. Wartime PWE assessments based on prisoner interrogations found a wide audience among German troops, and after D-Day more than half of the prisoners questioned said they had listened. Bomber Command's Arthur Harris dismissed the leaflet drops as useless. Later in life Delmer questioned whether his goals justified his methods, and his story was revisited in Peter Pomerantsev's 2024 book How to Win an Information War.
