## Overview

Operation Neptune was a 1964 disinformation operation in which Czechoslovak State Security (StB), with help from the Soviet KGB, staged the "discovery" of chests of Nazi documents at the bottom of Černé jezero (Black Lake) in the Šumava mountains of Bohemia. The find was presented as authentic wartime records in order to embarrass West Germany. It is known chiefly through Ladislav Bittman, the StB officer who ran it and later defected, and through declassified StB files.

## Background

West Germany's statute of limitations on Nazi war crimes was due to lapse in May 1965. According to StB records analysed by the historian Calder Walton, Neptune aimed to push Bonn into extending that deadline, to provoke an anti-German campaign in the West, and to disrupt West German intelligence work by naming Czechs who had supposedly collaborated with the Nazis. It was the first major test for Department D, a new active-measures unit of which Bittman became deputy chief in February 1964, overseen by KGB advisers.

## Methods

- A Czechoslovak television crew filming a documentary on the Šumava lakes provided the cover. In late spring 1964 the StB sank four chests in Black Lake; after about a week of searching, with Bittman posing as an Interior Ministry official, the crew's divers "found" them.
- The chests contained only blank paper. Once they reached Prague, the press reported that they held valuable Nazi documents.
- Because Czechoslovakia had few unpublished German records, the StB asked the KGB for material, and in September a courier brought documents from Moscow.
- According to Walton, the final dossier mixed genuine German records, including papers on the Anschluss and on German intelligence operations against Italy, with forgeries.
- On 15 September 1964 Interior Minister Lubomír Štrougal presented the "find" at a press conference in Prague, launching a campaign against selected West Germans.

## Exposure

The deception came to light only after Bittman defected to the United States in 1968. He described it in The Deception Game (1972), testified before Congress and later taught disinformation at Boston University. About 37 declassified StB files were later studied by Walton, who interviewed Bittman shortly before his death in 2018. Accounts differ on the papers: a 1985 Christian Science Monitor article based on Bittman's book called them real Nazi records, while Walton's reading of the files points to a mix of genuine and forged material.

## Legacy

West Germany extended the limitation period, which Czech Television presents as proof of success. Walton notes that the extension was probably coming anyway, and the Monitor concluded that the ploy worked only briefly; Bittman himself said it failed to convince Western Europeans that postwar West Germany was an outgrowth of Nazi Germany.
