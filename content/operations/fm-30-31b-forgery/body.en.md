## Overview

FM 30-31B was a forged U.S. Army document circulated by the Soviet bloc from the mid-1970s. Presented as a top-secret supplement to a real field manual, it purported to instruct Army intelligence to infiltrate radical groups in allied "host countries" and, if those governments seemed too passive towards communist subversion, to use such groups for violent actions that would convince them of the danger. A 1982 U.S. government report called it the most widely replayed Soviet forgery.

## Background

The Army did publish FM 30-31, "Stability Operations—Intelligence", dated January 1970, and a classified Supplement A. According to the U.S. government, a Supplement B never existed. The forgery was stamped "TOP SECRET" and carried the forged signature of General William Westmoreland, then Army Chief of Staff, hence its nickname, the Westmoreland Manual.

## Methods

The forgery followed the pattern U.S. analysts attributed to Soviet "active measures": an authentic-looking document, delivered anonymously and free of charge, then replayed in the press until its origin was obscured.

- In 1975 the Turkish newspaper Barış referred to the manual without publishing a facsimile.
- In 1976 a photocopy was left on the bulletin board of the Philippine Embassy in Bangkok with an anonymous note to President Ferdinand Marcos. Sources differ on the month.
- In September 1978 El País and the magazine Triunfo printed it in Spain. The 1980 congressional hearings attributed this to a Spanish communist and a Cuban intelligence officer.
- Replays followed in Italy, the Netherlands, Greece, Portugal, Mozambique and, in 1979, the United States.

Its greatest value came after the Red Brigades murdered the Italian Christian Democratic leader Aldo Moro in 1978, when Soviet propaganda used it to suggest U.S. links to left-wing terrorism. It was invoked again after the Red Brigades kidnapped U.S. General James Dozier.

## Exposure

In February 1980 the oversight subcommittee of the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence held hearings titled "Soviet Covert Action (The Forgery Offensive)", where the manual was described as a total fabrication. The 1982 report counted appearances in more than 20 countries and warned that good forgeries can be accepted long after being denounced.

In 1979, by contrast, the Covert Action Information Bulletin published the full text and argued it was genuine, citing its military phraseology and a U.S. Embassy official who had told an Italian publisher that printing it would be "inopportune".

## Legacy

After NATO's secret stay-behind networks, including Italy's Gladio, were revealed in 1990, the manual resurfaced as alleged proof that they had committed terrorism at U.S. instigation. In 2006 the State Department criticised a Greek writer and the Swiss researcher Daniele Ganser for treating it as authentic. The episode shows how a single forgery can outlive its exposure for decades.
