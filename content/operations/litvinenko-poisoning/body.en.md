## Overview

Alexander Litvinenko, a former KGB and FSB officer turned British citizen and Kremlin critic, was poisoned in London on 1 November 2006 with the radioactive isotope polonium-210 and died on 23 November. A British public inquiry concluded in 2016 that Andrey Lugovoy and Dmitry Kovtun poisoned him, probably under FSB direction, and that the operation was probably approved by the FSB's then director, Nikolai Patrushev, and by President Vladimir Putin. Both men deny involvement and Russia rejects the findings.

## Background

Litvinenko was dismissed from the FSB in 1998 after publicly alleging illegal activity inside the service, including a claim that he had been ordered to kill the businessman Boris Berezovsky. He left Russia in 2000, obtained asylum in Britain in 2001 and worked as a journalist and investigator. His books accused the FSB of the 1999 apartment bombings in Russia and of collusion with organised crime. The inquiry found that he was regarded inside the FSB as a traitor and that, according to Lugovoy, the FSB had been told he was working for British intelligence.

## Methods

According to the inquiry, Litvinenko ingested about 4.4 gigabecquerels of polonium-210 while drinking tea with Lugovoy and Kovtun at the Pine Bar of the Millennium Hotel in Mayfair; the highest contamination readings came from his table and the inside of a teapot. The inquiry chairman, Sir Robert Owen, also found an earlier attempt on 16 October 2006. Polonium-210 is produced in nuclear reactors, and the report called its use "at the very least a strong indicator of state involvement".

The killing also carried a message. The inquiry recorded evidence that Kovtun told an acquaintance in Hamburg that Litvinenko was to be poisoned rather than shot because "it is meant to set an example". The day after the death, a Russian Duma deputy told the chamber it would be "a serious warning to traitors".

## Exposure

Police examined more than 60 scenes and found polonium traces in hotels and aircraft used by Lugovoy. Britain sought Lugovoy's arrest for murder in 2007 and Kovtun's in 2011, but Russia does not extradite its citizens. Lugovoy became a Duma deputy and, in March 2015, during the inquiry's hearings, received a state honour from Putin.

In September 2021 the European Court of Human Rights, ruling in Carter v. Russia, a case brought by Litvinenko's widow, Marina, held that the two men had acted as agents of the Russian state. By six votes to one it found a violation of the right to life and awarded her 100,000 euros in damages. Russia had refused to take part in the inquiry or to give the court its investigation files.

## Legacy

Few state killings abroad have been examined in such detail by both a national inquiry and an international court. In Strasbourg, the widow's lawyers argued that it fitted a wider pattern that included the 2018 poisoning of Sergei Skripal in Salisbury.
