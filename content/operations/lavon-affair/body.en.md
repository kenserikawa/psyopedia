## Overview

The Lavon Affair grew out of Operation Susannah, a failed covert operation in which Israeli military intelligence used a cell of Egyptian Jews to plant incendiary devices at Egyptian, British and American civilian sites in Egypt in the summer of 1954. The attacks were meant to be blamed on the Muslim Brotherhood, communists or local nationalists. The cell was caught, two members were hanged, and a bitter dispute over who had given the order cost Defence Minister Pinhas Lavon his post and shook Israeli politics for years.

## Background

In 1954 Egypt was negotiating the withdrawal of British forces from the Suez Canal zone. According to the IDF Archives, the aim was to sabotage Egypt's relations with the West; documents released in 2015 show the hope was to keep British troops in Egypt by staging attacks that looked Egyptian-made. The operatives belonged to Unit 131, a small military intelligence unit commanded by Major Motke Ben-Zur, and had been trained as sleeper cells for wartime.

## Methods

- On 2 July 1954 the cell set fire to a post office in Alexandria; later attacks hit American libraries in Cairo and Alexandria.
- Cinemas were also targeted, and the devices were timed to ignite hours after closing time.
- Five simultaneous attacks were planned for 23 July, but operative Philip Natanson was arrested outside an Alexandria cinema when the device in his pocket caught fire.
- Material found in his flat led to the arrest of almost the entire network. Its commander, Avri Elad, escaped; he was widely believed to have betrayed the cell and was later jailed in Israel for contacts with Egyptian intelligence.

## Exposure

The trial in Cairo ran from 11 December 1954 to 27 January 1955. Moshe Marzouk and Shmuel Azar were hanged, others received long prison terms and two were acquitted; Max Bineth and Yosef Carmon died by suicide in custody. In Israel, military intelligence chief Colonel Binyamin Gibli said he had received a verbal order from Lavon, who denied it, while Prime Minister Moshe Sharett had apparently been kept in the dark. A two-member inquiry, made up of a Supreme Court justice and a former chief of staff, failed to establish the truth. Gibli and Ben-Zur were dismissed, and Lavon resigned in February 1955.

## Legacy

In December 1960 a new inquiry concluded that Lavon had not authorised the operation. David Ben-Gurion rejected the finding, and according to the IDF Archives the dispute eventually led to his resignation. The last prisoners reached Israel in February 1968. Israel long denied involvement, but in March 2005 President Moshe Katsav honoured the cell's members. Documents released in 2015, including the diary of Ben-Gurion's aide Nehemia Argov, who called the decision "criminal", still did not settle who gave the order.
