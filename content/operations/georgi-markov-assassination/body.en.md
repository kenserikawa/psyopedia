## Overview

Georgi Markov, a Bulgarian novelist and playwright who had become one of the communist regime's most effective critics in exile, was attacked at a bus stop by Waterloo Bridge in London on 7 September 1978 and died four days later. A pinhead-sized metal pellet recovered from his thigh pointed to ricin poisoning. The killing is widely attributed to Bulgaria's State Security service (Darzhavna Sigurnost, DS) with technical help from the Soviet KGB, but no one has ever been convicted.

## Background

Markov defected in 1969 and broadcast for Radio Free Europe, the BBC and Deutsche Welle, criticising the regime of party leader Todor Zhivkov. According to an RFE/RL reconstruction, Zhivkov told a Politburo meeting in 1977 that he wanted Markov silenced, and Interior Minister Dimitar Stoyanov asked the KGB for help. Documents later found in Bulgarian archives include a cooperation plan under which the KGB supplied devices for silently firing needles loaded with fast-acting poisons. The attack fell on Zhivkov's birthday.

## Methods

- A stranger jabbed Markov in the thigh, apparently with the tip of an umbrella, while he waited for a bus to work.
- The pellet, about 1.5 mm across, was made of platinum-iridium alloy and had two drilled holes, reportedly sealed with a coating that melted at body temperature.
- Porton Down scientists found the cavities empty; ricin was inferred from the symptoms and the minute dose the pellet could hold.
- RFE/RL reports earlier failed attempts in Munich and Sardinia. Fellow émigré journalist Vladimir Kostov survived a similar attack in the Paris metro, and an identical pellet was found in his body.
- The journalist Hristo Hristov argues that the umbrella was a distraction and that the real weapon was a pen-like injector.

## Exposure

In January 1979 a London inquest ruled that Markov had been unlawfully killed. After communism collapsed, the former KGB officers Oleg Kalugin and Oleg Gordievsky publicly acknowledged Soviet complicity, but many relevant Bulgarian files had been destroyed. Hristov won access to the DS archives after a six-year legal battle and identified agent "Piccadilly", Francesco Gullino, a Dane of Italian origin, as the probable assassin; according to the files, he travelled to London several times and was there on the day of the attack. Gullino was questioned in Denmark in 1993 and released for lack of evidence. He always denied involvement and died in Austria in 2021 without having been charged.

## Legacy

Bulgarian prosecutors closed their investigation in 2013, citing the statute of limitations, while the Metropolitan Police said its inquiry remained open. The pellet is kept in Scotland Yard's crime museum. The "Bulgarian umbrella" became a byword for the state-sponsored murder of exiles.
