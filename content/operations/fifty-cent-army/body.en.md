## Overview

"50 Cent Army", or 50 Cent Party (wumao in Chinese), is the popular name for people who post comments online at the direction of the Chinese authorities while presenting them as the spontaneous views of ordinary citizens. The nickname comes from a rumour that each post earned 50 Chinese cents. The most systematic study of the practice, by Gary King, Jennifer Pan and Margaret Roberts in the American Political Science Review (2017), estimated that the government fabricates about 448 million social media comments a year.

## Background

Evidence was long anecdotal, estimates reached two million commentators, and most observers assumed they argued the government's case. In December 2014 a blogger using the name Xiaolan released more than 2,700 emails from 2013 and 2014 belonging to the internet propaganda office of Zhanggong, a district of Ganzhou in Jiangxi province. According to Quartz, the emails listed nearly 300 commentators, many of whom pasted suggested talking points almost word for word to meet their quotas.

## Methods

King, Pan and Roberts extracted 43,757 posts from the archive and found that:

- More than 99% came from over 200 government agencies, and the authors were almost all government employees, not citizens paid per post. There was no evidence of the 50-cent payment.
- About half were comments on government websites, the rest mostly on Weibo and Baidu Tieba.
- Posts came in coordinated bursts, for example around the Qingming festival, a Communist Party plenum and riots in Shanshan, Xinjiang.
- Almost none argued with critics or touched controversial issues. Most were cheerleading for China, the Party's history and other regime symbols.

## Evidence

Extrapolating nationally, the researchers estimated about 448 million posts a year, 52.7% on government sites and roughly one in every 178 posts on commercial social media. They concluded that the aim was strategic distraction, changing the subject at sensitive moments rather than winning arguments, while cautioning that their results depended on the authenticity of the leaked archive and that other efforts might not follow the Zhanggong model.

Later leaks showed the system still in use. In December 2020 The New York Times and ProPublica reported on thousands of directives from the Hangzhou offices of the Cyberspace Administration of China. After the death of the doctor Li Wenliang in February 2020, censors activated fake commenters to flood social media with distracting chatter. Contractor software assigned tasks and calculated pay, and a Guangzhou document set piece rates, from 25 dollars for a long original post to one cent per repost.

## Legacy

The 2017 study replaced the image of mercenary trolls debating dissidents with that of officials producing praise on command. The 2020 documents add nuance: in some places commenters were paid by the task.
