## Overview

Zersetzung ("decomposition") was the East German Ministry for State Security's term for covert measures designed to break down people and groups it classed as "hostile-negative". Instead of arresting critics, the Stasi tried to discredit them, wreck their careers and relationships, and sow mistrust within their circles until they were isolated and paralysed. The dissident writer Jürgen Fuchs called it "quiet terror". The method is well documented because the Stasi's own files were opened after reunification.

## Background

According to the MfS lexicon of the German Federal Archives, open prosecution of dissidents became politically undesirable under Erich Honecker because the regime cared about its international reputation. An article published by the Federal Agency for Civic Education (bpb) describes a shift from 1972 towards "invisible" pressure. In January 1976 the Minister for State Security issued Directive No. 1/76 on "Operative Procedures" (Operative Vorgänge), which defined Zersetzung as a way of concluding a case. Targets were mostly independent peace, environmental and human-rights groups, people applying to emigrate, and active Christians.

## Methods

The directive named measures such as the "systematic discrediting of public reputation" using true and false information, and the "systematic organisation of professional and social failures" to undermine self-confidence. Documented techniques included:

- spreading rumours, for example about affairs, and stoking rivalry and suspicion within groups;
- anonymous letters, telegrams and phone calls, and compromising or staged photographs;
- covert entries into homes to move belongings, alongside bugging and wiretapping;
- summonses on false pretexts, professional bans, military call-ups and forced expatriation;
- using friends, colleagues and even spouses as informers.

Measures were usually combined in long campaigns. Detailed plans needed approval from senior officers, or from the minister or his deputy when organisations or prominent figures were targeted; the lexicon cites measures planned in 1983 against the Pankow Peace Circle. Officers studied what the Stasi called "operative psychology" at its law school in Potsdam, where doctoral theses addressed Zersetzung.

## Exposure

The Stasi Records Act came into force on 29 December 1991, and on 2 January 1992 the first people, among them Wolf Biermann, Jürgen Fuchs and Katja Havemann, inspected their files. Millions have since learned how the secret police intruded into their friendships, families and careers. The records, including Directive 1/76, are now held by the German Federal Archives.

## Legacy

No reliable count of Zersetzung victims exists. Estimates of people who suffered political persecution in East Germany between 1945 and 1989 range from 170,000 to over 300,000, but they cover all forms of repression. Clinical studies cited by the bpb describe lasting harm among those persecuted, including post-traumatic stress, depression and anxiety.
