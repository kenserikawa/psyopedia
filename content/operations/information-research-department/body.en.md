## Overview

The Information Research Department (IRD) was a semi-secret unit of the British Foreign Office that, from 1948 to 1977, gathered information on communist policy and propaganda and turned it into material for anti-communist publicity at home and abroad. Much of its output was unattributable: it reached journalists, writers, trade unionists and foreign governments without revealing that it came from the British state.

## Background

The IRD was created in 1948 under Clement Attlee's Labour government as part of its political warfare against the Soviet Union, and grew into one of the Foreign Office's largest departments in the early Cold War. According to The National Archives, its task was to collect information on "Communist policy, tactics and propaganda" and to supply material for anti-communist publicity. Responsibility passed to the new Foreign and Commonwealth Office in 1968.

## Methods

- Distributing briefings and articles through British missions and regional information offices, and working with the BBC, the Central Office of Information, the Ministry of Defence and allied governments, including the United States.
- Enlisting writers: files released in 1996 show it used figures such as Bertrand Russell, Stephen Spender and Arthur Koestler, promoted foreign-language editions of Orwell's Animal Farm and circulated the left-wing weekly Tribune to British missions.
- Working closely with the Trades Union Congress and, from 1951, running domestic propaganda through a secretive "English Section".
- Producing "black" propaganda: in 1965 and 1966 its monitoring unit in Singapore wrote a newsletter that posed as the work of Indonesian patriots and called for the Indonesian Communist Party to be eradicated while the army was killing suspected communists.
- In the 1970s, according to research by Giselle Gwinnett, producing anti-IRA propaganda during the Troubles and planting stories that supported Britain's bid to join the European Economic Community.

## Exposure

Much of what is known comes from its released records. In July 1996 files at the Public Record Office showed that George Orwell, ill with tuberculosis in 1949, had offered the IRD official Celia Kirwan a list of writers and journalists he considered "crypto-communists" or "fellow-travellers" who should not be trusted as propagandists. The FO 1110 series, transferred in 1995, now holds more than 2,600 files. In 2021 documents found at The National Archives by the researcher Nicholas Gilby formed the basis of Observer reports on the department's role in Indonesia.

## Legacy

Foreign Secretary David Owen closed the IRD in 1977. Gwinnett argues that the closure was driven by pressure for cost savings during an economic crisis rather than by embarrassment, and that its staff were absorbed into a new information department while unattributable work continued; The National Archives lists an Overseas Information Department as its successor until 1980.
